### E3. What is kept on the phone, and does it survive a new phone? (Asked 30 Oct 2026; decided 2 Nov 2026, see Decided. Kept for reference.)

**What this is about.** Everything the app keeps lives on the phone; there is no server. **Why it matters now.** I checked the code (30 Oct 2026): the app does not switch off Android's built-in backup. **The realistic options.**

1. **Keep Android's backup on, on purpose, for everything.** For the learner: Saved, History, chats, the profile and settings come back on a new phone; up to a day's changes may be missing. For the build: the same rules file with two exclusions. Cost: on a new phone the Home list and History start empty.
3. **Turn backup off.** Everything lives only on this phone. For the build: one line. Cost: a reinstall or a new phone starts from nothing, including months of saved points.

An export and import button in settings is possible later; it is not needed for the first build.

**My recommendation and why.** Option 1. It costs nothing, protects the long-term record D1 relies on, and is what already happens today, so the first build only makes it deliberate. If a learner later wants chats kept on the phone only, option 2 is a small change.

### E1. How big can one chat get before it ends? (Asked 28 Oct 2026; decided 29 Oct 2026, see Decided. Kept for reference.)

**What this is about.** B3 decided that a chat has one hard end: its size limit. When a chat reaches it, the partner signs off naturally ("tengo que irme... ¿Seguimos luego?") and the message field is replaced by "This chat has ended" and a New chat button. The soft goodbye at about ten exchanges comes well before that, and the learner can carry on after it. What is still open is the limit itself: what is counted, and how much.

**Why it matters now.** Each partner reply sends the whole current chat to the AI, so the limit is what stops a long chat from making every reply slower and dearer. B5 left single messages unlimited, so the limit is also the only thing that bounds a very long message. A coding agent needs one number and one rule to build it.

**The realistic options.**

1. **Count the characters in the whole chat, with a limit of about 20,000.** This counts what the learner and the partner have written together. As a guide, 20,000 characters is roughly 50 ordinary exchanges, about five times past the soft goodbye. For the learner: a normal chat never gets near it; only a long session, or a pasted wall of text, reaches it. For the build: before each reply the app adds up the characters; at the limit it asks the partner for its sign-off and closes the chat. That is a few lines of code and adds no waiting. Cost: at the limit, each reply sends about 5,000 tokens (pieces of text the AI is charged by). Most of that is the earlier part of the chat, which is charged at the reduced cached rate. Even on a dearer model that is around a tenth of a penny per reply.
2. **Count exchanges instead, for example 40 learner messages.** Easier to explain to a learner. Cost: with no limit on a single message, one long paste can make a short chat very large, so it bounds the cost less well. It is also not the character limit you proposed on 12 October.
3. **A tighter character limit, about 8,000 (roughly 20 exchanges).** Replies stay small and quick. Cost: a chatty learner reaches the end soon after the soft goodbye, which works against the low friction you wanted.

**My recommendation and why.** Option 1. It is your own idea from 12 October (each chat limited to a set number of characters), it bounds the cost even with unlimited single messages, and it is the simplest rule for an agent to build. The number can be changed later in one place, once you see how long your own chats run.

**What it decides.** What the chat's size limit counts and its starting value; that the partner's sign-off and "This chat has ended" are triggered when it is reached. Cost per chat, and which model the partner uses, are E2.

Answer: decided 29 Oct 2026: option 1 as put. Andrew first wrote "Limit of 200000", which was recorded as written; he then corrected it the same evening: "Sorry, I didn't mean 200,000. I meant 20,000." See E1 under Decided.

### C2. Does the partner say anything about the learner's Spanish during the chat? (Asked 26 Oct 2026; decided the same day, see Decided. Kept for reference.)

**Why it matters now.** It is one line in the partner's instructions, but it decides whether the learner hears about their Spanish from two places or one. If both the partner and the after-sitting check comment, they will sometimes disagree, and the learner will not know which to trust.

**The realistic options.**

For the learner: it feels like talking to a patient native speaker; the formal feedback still comes afterwards. For the build: two lines in the partner's instructions, no extra AI calls. Honest limit: the AI will do this unevenly (some slips used correctly in the reply, some not), and many learners will not notice a recast. That is acceptable under your P5 principle, because the after-sitting check is the real feedback.

2. **Grammar:** verb forms, tense and mood (including the subjunctive), agreement, ser and estar, por and para, the preposition a verb needs ("pensar en"), articles, pronouns, word order.
3. **Word meaning:** a word that says something other than what was meant (false friends such as "embarazada", "realizar" for "realise"; confused pairs such as saber and conocer).

Suggestions (second step):
4. **Natural phrasing:** understood, but not how a Spanish speaker would put it. Regional forms are never flagged in either group. Whether they appear as a neutral note is C4.

Word Choice is split by the test: if the word changes the meaning, the point goes under Word meaning (an Issue); if the word is understood but not the most natural, it goes under Natural phrasing (a Suggestion). The label "Word Choice" disappears.

### B3. How does a chat begin, carry on and end? (Decided 17 Oct 2026; see Decided. The question as asked and the mock-up record are kept below for reference.)

(Asked 8 Oct 2026.)

**Where things stand.** B2 made the home page the chat itself: it fills the screen under the three icons, with "New chat" at the top right. The mockup you accepted shows a chat already in progress, and I drew it on the assumption that the app reopens on the last chat. You have also said you want chatting to be as natural as possible, with little friction. Nothing in the app stores a conversation today. History keeps single submissions, each one text with its results (`lib/features/history/domain/correction_submission.dart`), and only the 20 most recent (`_recentLimit` in `lib/features/corrections/data/file_correction_repository.dart`). So chats need a store of their own whatever is decided here.

**Why it matters.** "A chat" has to mean something definite, because three later things hang on it. Feedback (stage C) needs a piece of the learner's language to look at, and you have proposed that feedback is offered after a chat. The partner's replies cost money in proportion to how much earlier conversation is sent with each one. And History has to list something the learner can reopen. The open choice is whether the learner has one never-ending thread, or a series of separate chats, and what they see when they open the app.

**The realistic options.**

1. **One continuous thread, as with a friend on WhatsApp.** There is a single conversation that never ends. Opening the app shows where it left off, with day markers between sessions. "New chat" is not needed; the learner or the partner simply changes the subject.
   - What the learner experiences: the most natural form. The partner appears to remember, and nothing has to be started or finished.
   - What has to be built: one growing conversation file; a rule for how many earlier turns are sent to the partner with each reply, since the whole thread cannot be sent for ever; and some other way of marking off a piece for feedback, such as "today's messages".
   - The weakness: there is no natural moment to offer feedback, and nothing tidy to list in History. A learner who was away for a fortnight comes back to a stale exchange and has to scroll past it.

2. **Separate chats, with the latest one kept open.** A chat is one conversation with a beginning and an end. Opening the app shows the current chat if it is still fresh (for example, started today), so the learner can carry on. If it is not fresh, the app opens a new chat, and the partner's opening question is already there. "New chat" closes the current one and opens another. Closed chats are listed in History and can be reopened to read.
   - What the learner experiences: close to the first option on any one day, since they can drop in and out. Each day, or each time they choose, starts clean with a new question.
   - What has to be built: a conversation model and store (a list of chats, each with its turns, language and dates); a rule for when a chat counts as closed; History showing chats alongside checked texts.
   - What it gives the later stages: a closed chat is the unit feedback is offered on, the unit stored, and the unit costed. Each reply sends only that chat's turns, so cost stays bounded.
   - The weakness: the partner does not remember earlier chats unless something is carried over. That carrying over is the guidance work of stage D (the record of difficulties, the profile, and later a short note of what was talked about), so it is postponed, not lost.

3. **Always a fresh chat.** Every time the app opens there is a new chat with a new question. Earlier chats are in History, and the learner can continue one from there.
   - What the learner experiences: always something new, never a stale screen.
   - What has to be built: the same as the second option, without the freshness rule.
   - The weakness: a learner who steps away for five minutes loses their place and has to go to History to get back. That is friction you have said you do not want.

**Who speaks first.** In the second and third options a new chat can open with the partner's question already showing, or empty. A question already there removes the blank page; it is the same point as a teacher giving the class a question and not just saying "talk to your partner". For the first build it can come from the bank of questions per interest that A1 already needs, so it appears at once and costs nothing. Later the partner can write it from the profile and from earlier chats. The learner can ignore it and say something else.

**Recommendation.** The second option, with the partner's question opening each new chat, and a chat counting as closed when the learner taps "New chat" or when a new day begins. It keeps the low friction you want inside a day, and gives a clear answer to "what do I get feedback on?" in stage C. From teaching you will recognise the shape: a fluency activity runs without interruption, and the look back at language comes afterwards, on a bounded piece of talk. A never-ending thread has no "afterwards".

The honest costs: a rule such as "a new day" will sometimes close a chat the learner wanted to continue at midnight or the next morning, so History needs a way to carry on an old chat, at least later. And the partner will seem forgetful between chats until stage D gives it something to remember with.

A different answer is welcome, including a mixture, for example the first option's single thread on screen with chats divided behind the scenes at each day marker.

**Decides:** the conversation model and its store; what the app shows on opening; what "New chat" does; whether a new chat opens with a partner question from the A1 bank; what History lists; the unit that stage C offers feedback on; how much earlier conversation is sent with each partner reply, which sets cost per chat in stage E.
